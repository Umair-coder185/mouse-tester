You miss a flick by an inch, then overcorrect on the next one. The problem is rarely your aim. It's usually a mismatch between your [mouse DPI](https://www.clickmousetest.com/) and in-game sensitivity. The best DPI and sensitivity settings for gaming start at 800 DPI with an eDPI that suits your game, then get tuned by feel. This guide shows you exactly how, with real pro data.

## DPI Only Controls Half of Your Aim

DPI, short for dots per inch, tells you how far your cursor travels for every inch you move the mouse. At 800 DPI, one inch of physical mouse movement sends the cursor 800 pixels across the screen. Manufacturers also use CPI (counts per inch) for the same measurement, so treat the two terms as identical.

However, DPI alone says little about how your aim feels. The game multiplies it by its own in-game sensitivity slider, and that product sets your real turning speed. Two players can run 400 DPI and 1600 DPI with matching slider values and still aim identically. That's where eDPI comes in.

## eDPI Is the Number That Lets You Compare Players

Effective DPI (eDPI) multiplies your mouse DPI by your in-game sensitivity. A player at 800 DPI and 0.5 sensitivity lands on 400 eDPI. So does a player at 1600 DPI and 0.25.

Quick Formula: eDPI = DPI × in-game sensitivity. Example: 800 × 0.4 = 320 eDPI.

Remember one rule, though: eDPI only compares players inside the same game. Every title uses a different rotation value, so 400 eDPI in Valorant feels nothing like 400 eDPI in CS2. Many guides skip this point, and it's why copied settings often feel wrong.

## cm/360 Shows What Your Arm Really Does

cm/360 measures how many centimeters you move the mouse to turn your character a full circle. It's the most honest sensitivity number because it ignores slider scales. The formula is cm/360 = (2.54 × 360) ÷ (DPI × sensitivity × yaw), where yaw is the game's rotation value.

According to CSDB.gg, yaw is 0.022 in CS2 and Apex Legends, 0.07 in Valorant and 0.0066 in Overwatch 2. For example, 800 DPI at 0.4 sensitivity in Valorant gives about 40.8 cm/360. In CS2, 800 DPI at 1.5 sensitivity gives about 34.6 cm/360.

## Best DPI and Sensitivity Settings for Gaming by Game

Each shooter rewards a different eDPI range. CSDB.gg tracks 1,007 professional CS2 players and reports a median of 800 eDPI. Valorant sits far lower: pro lists on Esports.gg and Gamers Decide show stars between roughly 176 and 576 eDPI.

You can convert between the two with one multiplier. CS2 eDPI × 0.314 equals Valorant eDPI, so 800 CS2 eDPI becomes about 251 in Valorant. Apex Legends, Overwatch 2 and Rainbow Six Siege follow the same logic through their own yaw values.

| Game | Yaw | Starting DPI | Starting eDPI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valorant | 0.07 | 800 | 240 to 400 |
| CS2 | 0.022 | 400 or 800 | 600 to 1200 |
| Apex Legends | 0.022 | 400 or 800 | Reuse your CS2 sensitivity directly |
| Overwatch 2 | 0.0066 | 800 | Convert from CS2 with the yaw ratio |
| Rainbow Six Siege | 0.00573 | 800 | Convert from CS2 with the yaw ratio |

Treat every row as a starting point, not a rule.

## Start at 800 DPI, Then Compare 400, 800 and 1600

Begin with 800 DPI. It keeps your in-game values easy to calculate and sits in the range most modern sensors handle well. Low-DPI loyalists still exist: CSDB.gg lists CS2 legend s1mple at 400 DPI with 3.09 sensitivity, an eDPI of 1236.

Higher DPI doesn't make your aim faster when eDPI stays equal. The gap between 400, 800 and 1600 DPI comes down to your sensor and your comfort. Pick one DPI, then adjust only the in-game slider.

| DPI | Who uses it | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| 400 DPI | s1mple in CS2 (3.09 sensitivity) | Needs higher in-game values; slow desktop cursor |
| 800 DPI | The easy baseline for every game | Needs decimals in low-sensitivity games |
| 1600 DPI | Hiko in Valorant (0.36 sensitivity, 576 eDPI) | Tiny in-game values; harder to fine-tune |

## DPI Won't Change Your FPS, but Your Hardware Will

DPI is a mouse setting, so it has no effect on frames per second. Your GPU and CPU control frame rate. To see where your system stands, run the [FPS Calculator](https://perfcalcpro.com/), then check for hardware limits with the [Bottleneck Calculator](https://perfcalcpro.com/).

Monitor resolution doesn't change your in-game turning speed either. Games rotate your view by degrees per mouse count, not by pixels. Resolution only changes how fast the desktop cursor feels, so a 1440p or 4K screen may suit 1200 to 1600 DPI on the desktop. Use a DPI preset button to switch between desktop and game DPI, and adjust your in-game sensitivity to keep the same eDPI.

## Seven Tips to Lock In the Best DPI and Sensitivity Settings for Gaming

Follow these tips in order to find your best DPI and sensitivity settings for gaming without guessing.

### Tip 1: Set an 800 DPI Baseline and Verify It

Set your mouse to 800 DPI in its software, then confirm the real number with the [DPI Analyzer](https://clickmousetest.com/). Some sensors report values slightly off from the label, so verifying removes a hidden variable. Choose 1200 to 1600 DPI only if you want a faster desktop cursor on a high-resolution display.

### Tip 2: Turn Off Mouse Acceleration Everywhere

Open the Windows mouse settings, switch off Enhance Pointer Precision and set pointer speed to 6 of 11, the 1:1 position. Next, disable any acceleration inside your game and enable raw input where it's offered. Without mouse acceleration, the same physical movement always gives the same result, which is the foundation of muscle memory.

### Tip 3: Choose a Target eDPI for Your Game

Use the table above to pick your starting eDPI. Valorant players can begin near 300, and CS2 players near 800. Divide the target by your DPI to get the slider value: 320 eDPI at 800 DPI means 0.4 sensitivity.

### Tip 4: Check Your cm/360 Against Your Desk Space

Calculate your cm/360 with the formula above. CSDB.gg reports most CS2 pros between roughly 35 and 75 cm/360, clustered around 45 to 60. A 40 cm/360 setting needs a mousepad at least 20 cm wide, and 30 cm or more feels better.

### Tip 5: Confirm Your Polling Rate

Set the polling rate to 1000Hz, which means the mouse reports its position 1000 times per second, then confirm it with the [Polling Rate Test](https://clickmousetest.com/). Rates such as 4000Hz and 8000Hz send more events to your CPU, so test them on your own PC before committing. Stable input latency beats a flashy number.

### Tip 6: Test in Short Sessions, Then Change by 5 to 10 Percent

Spend 20 minutes in a practice range or deathmatch with one setting. If you keep overshooting targets, lower your sensitivity by 5 to 10 percent. If you can't turn fast enough to face a flank, raise it by the same amount. Keep each setting for about a week before judging it.

### Tip 7: Match Your Aim Style to Your Setup

Low sensitivity pushes you toward arm aiming, with the wrist reserved for micro-adjustments. Higher sensitivity favors wrist aiming and faster flicks. Neither is wrong, but your desk space and mousepad must support the style you choose.

## Your Sensor and Switches Can Undermine Good Settings

Even perfect numbers fail on faulty hardware. A worn mouse sensor can cause jitter, and a failing switch can fire phantom clicks. Run the [Mouse Test](https://clickmousetest.com/) to check cursor smoothness and button response before you blame your sensitivity.

Double clicks are the most common switch fault. The [Double Click Test](https://clickmousetest.com/) shows you whether a button registers twice. Pair a healthy optical sensor with a clean, consistent mousepad surface, and your aim consistency stops depending on luck.

## Five Mistakes That Quietly Ruin Your Aim

Most sensitivity problems come from a few repeat errors. Avoid these:

* Treating one set of best DPI and sensitivity settings for gaming as universal for every title
* Copying a pro's slider value without matching their DPI and game
* Leaving acceleration on in Windows or inside the game
* Changing sensitivity every session instead of letting it settle
* Chasing a huge DPI number, when only your eDPI changes your aim

Fix these, and your muscle memory finally gets a stable target to learn.

## FAQ

### Is 800 DPI good for gaming?
Yes. 800 DPI is a practical baseline for every genre, and you tune the in-game slider from there.

### Is 1600 DPI too high for FPS games?
No, as long as your eDPI stays in range. Valorant's Hiko plays 1600 DPI at 0.36 sensitivity, an eDPI of 576.

### What is a good eDPI for Valorant?
Pro lists show stars between roughly 176 and 576 eDPI. A start near 300 suits most players.

### What is a good eDPI for CS2?
CSDB.gg shows a pro median of 800 eDPI, so 600 to 1200 is a sensible range.

### Does higher DPI reduce FPS?
No. DPI controls mouse sensitivity, while your GPU and CPU control frame rate.

### Is 400 DPI better than 800 DPI?
Not by itself. At the same eDPI, both give identical turning speed, so pick the one that feels comfortable.

## Pick Your Numbers, Then Stop Changing Them

The best DPI and sensitivity settings for gaming come down to one idea: fix your DPI, then tune your eDPI for the game you play. Start at 800 DPI, turn off acceleration, and use cm/360 to check that your desk has enough space. Pro data shows how wide the range is, from Valorant stars near 300 eDPI to CS2 pros around 800.

Your numbers are a starting point, not a verdict. Test one change at a time, give each a full week, and let muscle memory do its work. If your aim still feels off, rule out the hardware before you touch the sliders again.

Write down your DPI, sensitivity and cm/360, commit to them, and move on to improving crosshair placement and game sense. Stable settings free your attention for the parts of the game that actually win rounds.
